MBTA Communities/3A Compliance		 
	
		 
			

		   				
	 
	
	 	
 
	     


          The following Citizens&apos; Petitions addressing 3A compliance were submitted for inclusion in the June 9, 2025, Town Meeting warrant:    Lowell Road Multi-Family District    Site Plan Review Language    Zoning Map Effective Date    Town Meeting approved all three bylaws. The Attorney General&apos;s Office issued its approval of the bylaws on September 22, 2025. The Town&apos;s 3A compliance was subsequently reviewed by EOHLC, and a compliance letter was issued on October 3, 2025. Both letters are linked below:    AGO approval letter    EOHLC compliance letter    3A/MBTA Communities Background Information:  The following questions and answers are based on the most recent information the Town has about the 3A statute. The information is updated from time to time based on questions asked at public meetings and updates from Town Counsel. The answers contain the most recent information available to the Planning Department. For additional detail, please visit&nbsp; the opinion provided by Town Counsel (updated on September 6, 2024) .  What is the new &quot;MBTA Communities&quot; statute?  In Massachusetts, &nbsp;a new section 3A of the Zoning Act  was signed into law in January 2021. In 2022, the state released guidelines for communities explaining the new requirements. This new part of the Zoning Act requires cities and towns within the MBTA&apos;s service area, or &quot;MBTA communities,&quot; to have at least one zoning district that allows multifamily zoning by right.  Does this apply to North Reading?  The law includes MBTA adjacent communities, even if they have no service within their borders. North Reading falls into the state&apos;s category of &quot;adjacent community.&quot; While there is no MBTA service within the Town, the Town is adjacent to Wilmington, Reading, and Andover, all of which have MBTA commuter rail stations.  The state has published a map of MBTA Communities.   Does this mean we are getting MBTA service?  No. Complying with the law would require us to meet certain zoning requirements because the Town is within the general service area provided by the MBTA.  How did North Reading become an MBTA community?  A number of private companies operated various bus and trolley lines in the suburbs of Boston, including North Reading, up until 1964. In that year, the Commonwealth abolished the old Metropolitan Transit Authority, which had operated transit in Boston and the inner core cities and towns, and replaced it with the Massachusetts Bay Transportation Authority.  By an act of the Legislature in 1964 , the new MBTA subsumed the various transit systems that had existed in 64 suburbs, including North Reading.  From 1964 through the 1970s, the MBTA operated the 318 and 137 bus routes in North Reading intermittently, with changes, cuts, and experimental new additions to service due to changes in ridership. This included service from Lawrence to the Andover line, and also to the former Everett station at what used to be the northern end of the Orange Line.  The bus lines were eventually discontinued, but North Reading remains a community adjacent to MBTA commuter rail stations and has therefore paid an assessment to the MBTA for decades for this geographic privilege. A more detailed history of these assessments and of transit service in North Reading is provided  in this memo , which was prepared in part based on information from &nbsp;this article explaining the history of MBTA routes . Additional details can be found in this  Wikipedia article .  What would the Town need to do to meet the MBTA Communities requirements under 3A?  To meet the state&apos;s requirements, North Reading would need to have a zoning district of at least 50 acres (or multiple districts of 5 acres each, adding up to 50 acres; at least one of these must be 25+ acres). This district must allow multifamily housing by right, meaning it can&apos;t require a special permit for multifamily projects to be built.   The presentation from the Massachusetts Municipal Association gives further details about the law.   What is multifamily housing?  Multifamily is defined by the state as 3+ units per structure.  What are the other requirements for the district?  To comply with the law, the Town would need to make sure its zoning district could meet certain criteria for density if it were to be built out. This has nothing to do with what may be located in the district already. Zoning would be for a future scenario where the properties could be redeveloped. Under that scenario, we are asked to make certain calculations to be sure we meet the state&apos;s requirements. These include:   The minimum gross density is 15 units/acre.  The zoning needs to allow a capacity of at least 750 multifamily units by right (this does not mean 750 units need to be built, only that the district could provide for them if it were newly redeveloped).  Land needs to be developable to count (i.e., it cannot be floodplain, water, wetlands, or public land).  The zoning can&apos;t restrict age or the number of bedrooms. This does not mean the zoning district can&apos;t contain projects that happen to be age-restricted. It means the zoning can&apos;t require projects to be age-restricted.   What if the Town doesn&apos;t have the infrastructure to support new development? Are we expected to provide it?  The law is concerned with what the zoning allows, not what will be built. There is no requirement for towns to actually produce the housing associated with this zoning or to provide infrastructure to service it.  What if the Town does not comply?  MA state law requires the Town to have a district that complies with the requirements of 3A. The state&apos;s ability to enforce this is currently being contested in court. Town Counsel has provided the Town with information from the current case concerning the Town of Milton, including the  Attorney General&apos;s complaint , an  affidavit of the director of the Division of Community Services for the Commonwealth&apos;s Executive Office of Housing and Livable Communities , and the  Town of Milton&apos;s counterclaim . ( Milton&apos;s Appellee brief. ) Additionally, counsel has provided the Town with amicus briefs filed on behalf of the towns of  Hamilton&nbsp; and  Middleborough .  A consequence of not complying with the new statute would be becoming ineligible for funding for several of the state&apos;s grant programs. For others, compliance is considered when the state makes its funding decisions.  This linked spreadsheet lists the grant programs affected , describes each, specifies whether the Town has used the program in recent years, and how much was received.  Additionally, the  Attorney General&apos;s office released this linked advisory  in March of 202,3 clarifying the State&apos;s position on compliance.  What is North Reading&apos;s deadline for compliance?  December 31, 2024.  How is the Town proposing to meet this requirement?  The Community Planning Commission and Select Board have discussed a zoning overlay proposal that utilizes properties that are already zoned for multifamily housing by right, with no age restrictions, at a density level and with several units that already exceed the state&apos;s requirements. 100 Lowell Road (site of Edgewood Apartments) and 104 Lowell Road/Berry Way (site of the Residences at Martins Landing) have already been permitted and are in the process of being built out with housing developments that closely mirror the requirements in the proposed new overlay zoning district. This proposal minimizes the actual physical changes that could be made as a result of meeting the state&apos;s requirements. It was proposed as the least impactful solution possible that still would comply with the new law. Note that, as with all zoning changes, actual physical changes to the site require the owner(s) to make an application to the Town and receive all permits before any construction.  Three zoning bylaw changes are proposed:   An article to establish an overlay district containing 100 and 104 Lowell Road. Within that overlay, the proposed number of stories is 4.5 rather than the currently allowed 4.0. This adjustment allows us to meet the state&apos;s &quot;unit capacity&quot; requirements. The height limit currently in place, 60 feet, would remain unchanged. For new/redevelopments of 10 or more units, 10% of the new units must be affordable.  The text of the bylaw is attached .  An article to clarify site plan review procedures, establishing that they act like a special permit but are not true special permits. The state will not allow us to require a special use permit in this district, and we need to make it clear that we are not requiring one. &nbsp;The text of this bylaw amendment is attached in a document.  To see how the final zoning text would look after the changes,  the redlined changes can be viewed.   An article to update the date of the Town&apos;s zoning map, which needs to be done whenever a map amendment occurs.  The text of this bylaw amendment is attached in a document.    What are the other possible impacts on these two sites?  100 and 104 Lowell Road are both quite restricted due to being located in an Aquifer Protection District, and this is not proposed to change. The APD limits by-right development to 15% impervious surface. Beyond that point, a special permit is needed. Both developments already exceed that threshold based on previous special allowances for when those projects were approved, and for that reason, the new zoning could not enable development that expands the buildings&apos; footprints. Rather, the only way to expand on the current developments under the new zoning would be to build upward, or to rebuild the sites anew. The height restriction for these properties is currently 60 feet and four stories. The Edgewood Apartments have three stories, while Martins Landing has a combination of four and five-story buildings.   How are the owners of 100 and 104 Lowell Road impacted by this? ( Linked to the full Town Council opinion (updated September 6, 2024 ), which addresses impacts to Martins Landing.   The following is excerpted from Town Counsel&apos;s communication of 9/6/24, linked in full above:  Would placing the Martins Landing property within the MBTA communities zoning district eliminate or negate the 55+ requirement applicable to that development?   No. In my opinion, the inclusion of an existing 55+ residential development, such as Martins Landin, in an MBTA overlay district would not eliminate or negate the 55+ requirement applicable to those developments. MBTA zoning requires that multi-family housing be allowed by right without age restrictions in a zoning district of a certain size and capacity, but it does not otherwise govern what uses may be allowed or prohibited in the district. Thus, an MBTA zoning district that allowed multi-family housing by right without age restriction and had sufficient area and capacity to allow development of unrestricted multi-family housing in quantities meeting MBTA zoning requirements could continue to allow age-restricted housing by right or by special permit in the district provided that unrestricted multi-family housing is allowed by right throughout the district regardless of what other uses have or may be permitted. Alternatively, if the Town chose, an MBTA district could prohibit age-restricted development, but because zoning changes do not apply to existing uses and structures, such a prohibition would not apply to existing age-restricted developments in the district. If the new MBTA Zoning Bylaw/overlay district prohibited 55+ residential use, the existing 55+ use in the district could continue as a lawful pre-existing nonconforming use. [Excerpted from KP opinion of 9/6/24, linked above]   How can the proposed zoning allow 4.5 stories? What&apos;s half a story?  An example of a half-story would be a top floor that had a sloped roof and no dormers. It would be estimated to have about half the units of a full story.  Why is the state asking us to raise the height limit allowed for these buildings?  The state doesn&apos;t specifically have any particular height requirement. However, for this site, it would be the only way to meet the required &quot;unit capacity&quot; of 750 units. This is because building outward and expanding building footprints is restricted on this site due to its location in the Aquifer Protection District. No more impervious surface can be added to the site without a new special permit. All future development estimated for purposes of this law needs to be under the assumption that no special permits are needed.  Is the Town being asked to add 750 units?  No. We are asked to make sure our zoning district has enough capacity, based on by-right zoning, for 750 units. The by-right zoning on the properties today yields an estimate of just over 700 units, according to the state&apos;s formulas. However, the actual sites are already permitted for a total of 908 units. This is because the sites, at the time they were permitted, were given special permits, beyond the by-right zoning, to allow for more units. The state is not asking, nor is the Town proposing, to add 750 units on top of this. The 750 figure is simply an estimated capacity threshold for what is allowed in the zoning. There is no expectation, nor would there be room on the sites, to add 750 units to the 908 already permitted.  Does the zoning proposal mean the remaining two buildings at Martins Landing will be different from what was permitted?  Martins Landing will be constructing its remaining two buildings as previously permitted. They will be five stories, as approved. Like the rest of the development, these residences will be 55+.  If the Town rezones 100 and 104 Lowell Road, is the new zoning permanent?  The Town has been advised by counsel that the Town Meeting can vote to rezone or change existing zoning at any time, including to repeal previously approved zoning districts. The quantum of votes for passing the zoning is an ample majority; the quantum of votes for repealing it would be 2/3.  If the Town rezones this district to comply with the MBTA Communities law, does the Town then hand over control of the zoning or of the property to the state?  The properties remain under the control of their owners. The new zoning district, like every zoning district in the Town, is subject to the Town&apos;s zoning bylaws. The state does not assume any control over the property.  How do we know the proposal will be acceptable to the state?  The state has provided a &quot;compliance model&quot; for communities to use to be sure we are meeting all of the requirements. This is a spreadsheet for us to input all of our zoning information, and the model&apos;s formulas tell us whether we meet the required numbers for density, number of units allowed, and other factors.  This is a link to the completed compliance model North Reading is using for its proposed district.   The state&apos;s Executive Office of Housing and Livable Communities (EOHLC) has completed a test review of the compliance model and draft zoning bylaws. As a result of this review ( the feedback letter is attached ), we were asked to do an additional calculation to show we were not including any floodplain in our developable area and also to review our site plan review bylaw carefully with Town Counsel to be sure it met the requirements. This additional work has been done, and we are still able to demonstrate compliance.  Additionally, the Attorney General&apos;s municipal services unit did a preliminary review of the proposed zoning bylaws and issued  this feedback letter  (the change suggested has been incorporated into the zoning proposal).  Can the CPC still ask new projects to come in for a Site Plan Review?  Yes, the requirement to go through a Site Plan Review remains unchanged.  Are there affordable units required as part of this new zoning?  For additions or reconstruction projects involving 10 or more units, the zoning will require 10% of those new units to be affordable. Currently, the Edgewood Apartments are 25% affordable, and Martins Landing is 1.5% affordable. The 10% requirement is a way for the Town to keep pace with its 40B requirements in case the sites are added to or redeveloped in the future.  Why are we only requiring 10% of any new units to be affordable? Isn&apos;t this an opportunity to introduce more affordability?  The law does not require towns to require any affordability in zoning districts. It does allow towns to require up to 10% affordable housing. If we wanted to require anything more, we would need to perform an economic feasibility analysis to show that such a requirement would be economically realistic for developers. This is an option for the future, if the Town wishes to pursue it. However, we would need to perform the analysis and then receive approval from the state to make the change.  How does this relate to Chapter 40B?  This is a separate law from Chapter 40B, which requires communities to have at least 10% subsidized housing in their existing housing stock. As noted, there are no affordability requirements that the state is imposing under this law. The presence of affordable housing projects elsewhere in the Town has no relationship to this zoning district.  What are the next steps?  The CPC submitted the zoning amendments for consideration at the June 10, 2024, Town Meeting. On that date, Town Meeting voted to refer the matter to committee (meaning to the CPC), for further discussion, and with a commitment to bring the amendments back to October TM for consideration. The articles were placed on the warrant for the October 7, 202,4, Town Meeting. The zoning articles for 3A compliance did not pass at the October 7, 20,24, Town Meeting.  Similar articles have been submitted by Citizens&apos; Petition for the June 9, 2,0,25 Town Meeting. If the zoning proposal receives a positive vote at Town Meeting, the Town will then submit the approved bylaws, zoning map, compliancemodelml and application form to EOHLC for the state&apos;s approval.  How is the Town communicating with residents about this law and the proposed zoning?  Over the past two years, the CPC has had discussions at public meetings and at Select Board meetings as plans were developed. A pre-adoption test review was done by the state, and the AGO made comments on the draft zoning articles. At that point, the CPC discussed the proposal more fully at their meetings, and on March 25, 2024, the CPC and Select Board met jointly to discuss the proposal. &nbsp;This presentation was given.  Town Counsel was present in order to address many questions that were raised by the Select Board and CPC members. Before the meeting, Town Counsel provided a written opinion addressing some of these questions;  this opinion was also discussed and is attached . An updated version of this FAQ was issued on September 6 and is  attached .   The meeting was broadcast on NORCAM (visit 5 min 00 sec).
